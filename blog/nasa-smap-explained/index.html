NASA SMAP, Explained for People Who Do Not Work at NASA

April 2026

← Back to Blog

SMAP stands for Soil Moisture Active Passive. It is a NASA satellite launched in January 2015, and it does something that sounds impossible: it measures how wet the ground is from space.

How you measure dirt from orbit

Water changes how soil interacts with microwave radiation. Wet soil and dry soil emit measurably different signals in a specific band of the spectrum, and those signals pass through clouds and darkness. SMAP detects that emission with a radiometer and converts it into an estimate of moisture in the top few centimeters of soil.

The satellite passes over most of the planet every two to three days, which means the record is not a snapshot but a time series stretching back a decade.

What it is good at, and what it is not

SMAP is excellent at scale and consistency. It is less precise at the level of a single field, because each measurement covers a large area and averages everything inside it. Dense forest canopy, frozen ground, and urban surfaces all complicate the reading.

That tradeoff is why satellite data works best as one input among several rather than the whole answer. Combined with local weather, crop stage, and soil type, it becomes a strong foundation for irrigation modeling.

The part that surprises students

All of it is public. Anyone with an internet connection can download SMAP products and start analyzing them. The barrier is not access. It is knowing the data exists and having the skills to work with it, which is a much more solvable problem.
